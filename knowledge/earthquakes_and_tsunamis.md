# Earthquakes

An earthquake is the shaking of the ground caused by a sudden release of energy inside the Earth. Most earthquakes happen where tectonic plates meet. The plates are huge slabs of rock that make up the Earth's outer shell, and they move very slowly, a few centimetres a year, about as fast as your fingernails grow. When plates get stuck and then suddenly slip, the stored energy travels through the ground as seismic waves.

The place underground where an earthquake starts is called the focus or hypocentre. The point on the surface directly above it is the epicentre. Scientists measure earthquakes with seismographs.

## How earthquakes are measured

The magnitude of an earthquake is usually given on the moment magnitude scale, which replaced the older Richter scale. Each whole number step means about 32 times more energy released. A magnitude 5 earthquake is felt by most people and may crack walls; a magnitude 7 can destroy buildings; a magnitude 9 is among the largest ever recorded. Around 500,000 earthquakes happen every year, but only about 100 cause damage, and most are too small to feel.

## Aftershocks and foreshocks

After a big earthquake there are usually smaller earthquakes called aftershocks, which can go on for days or weeks. Sometimes smaller earthquakes called foreshocks happen before the main one, but scientists cannot use them to predict exactly when a big earthquake will strike. Earthquakes cannot be predicted precisely, which is why preparation matters.

## Staying safe in an earthquake

The safest thing to do indoors is Drop, Cover, and Hold On: drop to the ground, take cover under a sturdy table or desk, and hold on until the shaking stops. Stay away from windows and heavy furniture. If you are outside, move to an open area away from buildings, trees, and power lines. Do not run outside during the shaking, because falling objects near buildings are a big danger.

# Tsunamis

A tsunami is a series of very large ocean waves, most often caused by an undersea earthquake that suddenly lifts or drops the sea floor. Landslides and volcanic eruptions under the sea can also cause them. In the open ocean a tsunami wave can be less than a metre high and travel as fast as a jet plane, around 800 kilometres per hour. When it reaches shallow water near the coast it slows down and grows much taller, sometimes over 30 metres.

A tsunami is not one wave but several, and the first wave is often not the biggest. Sometimes the sea pulls back from the shore just before a tsunami arrives, which is a warning sign to move to high ground immediately. The 2004 Indian Ocean tsunami and the 2011 tsunami in Japan were two of the most serious in recent history, and they led to better warning systems around the world.

## Tsunami warning systems

Tsunami warning centres use seismographs and special buoys in the ocean called DART buoys that detect changes in water pressure. When a warning is issued, sirens and phone alerts tell people on the coast to move to higher ground. If you are near the coast and feel a strong earthquake, do not wait for a warning; move inland or uphill right away.
