# Hurricanes, cyclones, and typhoons

Hurricanes, cyclones, and typhoons are the same kind of storm with different names depending on where they happen: hurricanes in the Atlantic and north-east Pacific, typhoons in the north-west Pacific near Asia, and cyclones in the Indian Ocean and South Pacific. Scientists call all of them tropical cyclones.

They form over warm ocean water, at least about 27 degrees Celsius, where warm moist air rises and creates a spinning area of low pressure. The spin comes from the rotation of the Earth. The calm centre of the storm is called the eye, and the strongest winds are in the eye wall around it. Tropical cyclones bring very strong winds, heavy rain, and a storm surge, which is a wall of sea water pushed onto land by the wind. Storm surge causes most of the deaths in these storms.

## Hurricane categories and names

Hurricanes are rated from category 1 to category 5 on the Saffir-Simpson scale based on wind speed. Category 1 winds start at about 119 kilometres per hour, and category 5 winds are over 252 kilometres per hour. Storms are given human names from a list that repeats every six years so that forecasters and the public can talk about them clearly. Names of very destructive storms are retired.

# Tornadoes

A tornado is a violently spinning column of air that reaches from a thunderstorm cloud to the ground. Tornadoes are much smaller than hurricanes, usually less than a few hundred metres wide, but their winds can be even faster, over 300 kilometres per hour in the strongest ones. They are rated on the Enhanced Fujita scale from EF0 to EF5. The central United States, sometimes called Tornado Alley, has more tornadoes than anywhere else, but tornadoes happen on every continent except Antarctica.

If a tornado warning is issued, go to the lowest floor of a sturdy building, in a small inside room like a bathroom or closet, away from windows. A basement or storm shelter is safest. Cars and mobile homes are not safe places during a tornado.

# Floods

A flood happens when water covers land that is normally dry. Floods can be caused by heavy or long-lasting rain, rivers overflowing, snow melting quickly, dams breaking, or storm surge from the sea. Flash floods happen very quickly, sometimes within minutes, often in steep areas or cities where water cannot soak into the ground. Floods are the most common natural disaster in the world.

Moving flood water is extremely dangerous. Just 15 centimetres of fast-moving water can knock a person over, and 60 centimetres can carry away a car. Never walk or drive through flood water. Flood water may also be polluted with sewage and chemicals, so avoid touching it and wash well afterwards. People can prepare by knowing whether they live in a flood zone, keeping important documents high up, and having an evacuation plan.

# Droughts and heatwaves

A drought is a long period, months or years, with much less rain than normal. Droughts develop slowly, which makes them different from sudden disasters like earthquakes. They cause crops to fail, rivers and reservoirs to dry up, and can lead to food and water shortages and wildfires. Saving water, growing crops that need less water, and storing water in wet years all help communities cope with drought.

A heatwave is a period of unusually hot weather lasting several days. Heat can be dangerous, especially for young children, older people, and pets. During a heatwave, drink plenty of water, stay in the shade or in cool buildings during the hottest part of the day, and never leave people or animals in parked cars.

# Wildfires

A wildfire is an uncontrolled fire in a forest, grassland, or bush. Wildfires need three things, called the fire triangle: fuel such as dry plants, oxygen, and heat to start it. Lightning starts some wildfires, but most are started by people, by accident or on purpose. Hot, dry, windy weather makes wildfires spread faster.

Wildfires destroy homes and animal habitats and create smoke that can make air unhealthy hundreds of kilometres away. However, some forests actually depend on fire; certain pine trees only release their seeds after the heat of a fire. Firefighters use water, fire retardant dropped from aircraft, and firebreaks, which are strips of land cleared of plants, to stop fires spreading. If you live in a wildfire area, families keep a go-bag ready and follow evacuation orders quickly.

# Climate change and natural disasters

Climate change means the long-term warming of the Earth caused mainly by people burning fossil fuels such as coal, oil, and gas, which release greenhouse gases. Warmer air holds more moisture, so heavy rain and floods can become more intense. Warmer oceans give tropical cyclones more energy. Heatwaves, droughts, and wildfires are becoming more frequent in many regions. Climate change does not cause earthquakes or volcanic eruptions, because those come from inside the Earth, but rising sea levels can make coastal flooding and storm surge worse.
